# The Eroticization Operator

**"Sexual" — is it a content category the brain detects, or a value the brain assigns to ordinary
world-representations?** 531 self-attacking rounds against one public dataset, asking what can be
settled before anyone collects anything new. **486 numbered ledger entries** — [RETRACTIONS.md](RETRACTIONS.md)
carries the history; this page carries only what currently stands. **中文版:[`README_zh.md`](README_zh.md)** — English is canonical; corrections land here first.

Private curiosity project. Not written for publication and not seeking any.

Object: the **Big Kink Survey** public subsample — [Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.18625141](https://zenodo.org/records/18625141),
15,503 respondents × 365 columns, aggressively binned, demographically stripped, noise-injected.
Data is not committed — neither the raw release nor the person-level derived tables; `E01…/A01…/R001_schema/run.py` re-downloads and rebuilds them. **[CORRECTED before publication]** 24 derived `data/derived/*.csv` files, two of them with one row per respondent, were tracked until now, so this sentence was false; they are untracked and gitignored as of this commit. They remain in the commit history.

> ### How big is any of this? (`#264`, read this before anything else)
> Every person-level dimension this project established, **entered together** against the same 29
> outcomes, explains a **median of 1.4%** of the variance — **7.8% at its best** (`animated`), **2.8%**
> for shame. The degrees-of-freedom floor is **0.14%**, and the same pipeline reaches **49%** when a
> deliberately strong predictor is added, so the machinery is not blind and the dimensions are not
> noise: they are real, replicable, cross-instrument, controlled — **and they are small**.
> **Two readings this does not license.** It does *not* mean the findings are empty (the floor is
> 0.14%, and every dimension clears its own family-wise threshold). It does *not* mean sexual
> preference is mostly random — **1.4% is the reach of *these six coordinates*, not a ceiling on
> explainability**. ⚠ And note the ordering: the *least reliable* standing dimension (position, 0.432)
> contributes the *largest* incremental R² (0.715%). **Reliability and explanatory power are different
> things — a quantity can stably measure something that hardly moves anything.**
> ⚠ **`#301` `#302`:**in-sample 1.4% / out-of-sample 0.87%**. Held out — 5-fold person-level cross-validation with the six coordinates **re-estimated inside every fold** — the same pipeline delivers a median of **0.869%**; with the coordinates held fixed it is 1.087%, and ridge-CV 1.056%. On 2 of the 29 outcomes the held-out R² is **negative**. **Over a third of the published number is fit.** The estimator is the largest single knob in [`CALIBER.md`](CALIBER.md) (**0.52pp**, against 0.18pp for every analysis knob). Both numbers are legitimate and they answer different questions — 1.390% is what these coordinates explain **in the people they were built from**, 0.869% is what they deliver **on people they have never seen**. ⚠ **`#356`: and part of that 1.4% is co-linear with how much a person reported.** Adding "how many onset categories you filled in" to the six coordinates and doing a commonality decomposition across all 29 outcomes, the shared component is positive for **22 of 29** outcomes, median **0.345pp** — **18.2% of the median outcome's explained variance** — and it grows with how well the outcome is explained (`r` = **+0.609**). Swapping the breadth proxy for block coverage keeps it (25/29 positive; the two shared vectors correlate **+0.892**). ⚠ Commonality is a bookkeeping term, not a causal one: it says this part is shared, not which side carries it.

## The three models being separated

Ivan's framing, kept verbatim because the whole repository is scoped by it:

| | |
|---|---|
| **A** dedicated sexual-content system | `Y_i(s) = f_i(h_sex(s))` — "sexual" is a content category like *face* in vision |
| **B** erotic valuation of ordinary representation | `v_i(s,c,t) = w_i(c,t)^T h(s)` — ordinary scene, a readout applied to it |
| **C** recursive mix | value re-enters and reshapes the representation it acted on |
> ### Where the three models stand (`#234c`)
> **B is dead** (`#179`: position is not a stance-free coordinate — it comes with shame).
> **C's one distinguishing prediction is unsupported** (`#234a`: stratify by shame, match pick count,
> and the breadth profile's split-half replication is **0.4672 / 0.4611 / 0.4718** — flat, in a design
> whose positive control fires at a gradient **7.4× smaller** than the one C predicts).
> **But the honest reading is not "A wins."** The most reliable person-level structure in this release —
> split-half **0.56–0.72**, against the position score's 0.432 — is *which domains a person opens in and
> in what shape* (`#229`–`#232`). **None of A, B or C predicts it**, because all three ask what makes a
> thing sexual, and this is a fact about the **person**, not the stimulus.
> **The A/B/C decomposition does not cut this data at its joints.** That is a claim about the
> decomposition's completeness, not a refutation of A.

# A second instrument, and one thing it settles

Everything above is one survey. Two public datasets were added 2026-08-05 so that a claim can be
asked of an instrument that does not share this one's method: **the GSS** (US probability samples,
1972–2024) and **the SCCS** (186 ethnographically coded societies). Neither can be pushed; both were
read as they are.

**In the US, condemnation of pornography collapsed and the gap it marks did not move.** Among adults
aged 25–54, the share saying pornography should be illegal for everyone fell **0.38 → 0.18** across
30 surveys. The log-odds gap between condemners and non-condemners in *reporting* having seen an
X-rated film in the past year was **−1.34** in 1973–1988 and **−1.44** in 2006–2024:
**Δ = −0.10, 95% CI [−0.29, +0.09]**, against a **minimum detectable effect of 0.27** — so a shift
of the size that would matter (0.5) would have been seen, and was not. Across a 36-cell grid
(3 age bands × 3 era widths × 2 statistics × with/without the 2021+ mode change) **35 of 36 cells
carry the same sign and 3 exceed the MDE, all of them in the 35–64 band**, in the direction of the
gap getting slightly *wider*.

**What that rules out, and what it does not.** It rules out the simplest version of "shame suppresses
the report": if the collapse of condemnation had been releasing a concealed population, the gap would
have narrowed, and at this resolution it would have been visible. ⚠ It does **not** rule out
concealment in general — **exposure opportunity was not held fixed**. Availability of the material
changed enormously over the same 51 years, and if that change was asymmetric between condemners and
non-condemners, a real narrowing could be masked by it. The claim is about **the era contrast at
fixed life stage in this instrument**, not about concealment as such.

**And the item it was measured with is not the only one the survey uses.** In 2022 and 2024 the GSS
split its sample at random between two behaviour questions — *"seen an x-rated movie in the last
year"* (yes/no) and *"seen pornography in the past year"* (a ten-point frequency scale). The arms are
balanced (max |z| = 1.95 over five demographics). They do not report the same thing: **0.320 vs
0.425 positive, Δ = +0.105, 95% CI [+0.079, +0.135]** — a tenth of the population moves in or out of
"has seen any" depending on which question they were handed. The **condemnation gap itself**, though,
is not distinguishable between the arms: **Δ log-odds = −0.27, 95% CI [−0.61, +0.08]**, against an
MDE of **0.50**. ⚠ Two things vary together between the arms (the object named, and the answer
format), so that difference cannot be attributed to either alone; and because both arms are
self-report in the same questionnaire, their **agreement licenses nothing** — only the bound
**|Δ| < 0.50** is reportable. Across the frequency scale's eight cut points and four subgroups,
**12 of 12 cells put the gap on the same side**, and it widens monotonically as the cut moves toward
heavy use (−1.57 at *several times a year* to −2.19 at *every week*).

of 0.20 is between a fifth and a quarter of what changing the topic does inside a single
questionnaire. **The "same size" therefore carries no information at all**: it is smaller than the
noise that swapping one word introduces, and it should not be read as cross-instrument agreement.

**Is "the gap" one number?** No — and the sharpest case differs by a single word. Inside one NSFG
questionnaire, one sample, one mode, six attitude/behaviour pairs give log-odds from **−0.47 to
−1.33**. Three of them were specified before being computed: *divorce is the best solution*
(−0.77), *all right for unmarried **18**-year-olds to have sex* (−0.52), *all right for unmarried
**16**-year-olds* (−1.07). **The last two are asked of the same people about the same behaviour —
first intercourse at 16 or younger — and differ only in the age printed in the question.** Their
intervals do not overlap ([−0.64, −0.41] vs [−1.25, −0.90]). ⚠ The share who condemn also differs
sharply (43% vs 85%), so who counts as a "condemner" is not held fixed; across all six pairs the
condemnation share does not order the gaps monotonically, but that is an observation, not a test.
⚠ And a wide interval favours "they overlap", so a disjoint pair is found in the conservative
direction.

**And it is not carried by how many people condemn.** Across the six topics, the gap does track the
share who condemn — slope **+0.60, 95% CI [+0.27, +0.93]**. But that association is confounded: the
share and the topic vary together. Holding the topic and the behaviour fixed and moving only the
threshold for counting as a condemner (strongly disagree / disagree / mildly-or-worse) gives a
within-topic slope of **−0.18, CI [−0.39, +0.01]**, against an **MDE of 0.28** — a design that could
comfortably have seen +0.60 and did not. **So the across-topic relation is carried by the topics, not
by the headcount.** ⚠ Moving the threshold changes the *intensity* of the condemning group as well as
its size, so "size does not matter" is entangled with "intensity does not matter"; and — this is the larger caveat — **that pooled slope is an average over topics that genuinely
disagree**. Fitting one slope per topic gives **−0.61, −0.68, −0.35** for same-sex relations,
cohabitation and 18-year-olds, against **+0.43, +0.23, +0.01** for divorce, unmarried childbearing and
16-year-olds; a heterogeneity test against a null in which all six share one slope gives **Q = 22.3,
p = 0.001**, and no single topic drives it (leave-one-out Q stays at 12.7–21.9). **So "the threshold
does not matter" holds only on average, and on average is not the same as everywhere.** ⚠ Q's own
resampling precision is UNCOMPUTED.

**One person-level coordinate does travel across topics — but it is strictness, not position.** In the
NSFG, counting how many of eight sexual/family norms a woman takes the *strict* side on gives a number
that predicts, for each of five behaviours, whether she has done it — **after** controlling for her
attitude on that very norm and for how many norms she answered. Per additional strict position the
probability falls by about **0.019–0.027** (four of five behaviours, each beyond its own MDE), and a
sham coordinate built from extreme-response style alone is weaker in four of five. ⚠ An earlier
version of this coordinate counted "minority side" rather than "strict side" and was **incoherent** —
on seven of eleven items the minority side *was* the strict side, on the rest it was the permissive
one; that version is retracted. ⚠ This is **not** the rarity-position coordinate of the first half of
this page: it measures how strict you are, not how unusual your tastes are. NSFG has no shame item, so
"position → shame" cannot be replicated there at all. `[#500, #501]`

**And that strictness coordinate is not one ruler but two** `[#523「一个新数字也没有引入」]`**.** Splitting the eight norms into three about
sexual conduct and five about marriage and childrearing, and entering both counts together, the
coefficient for each behaviour is carried by its **own** domain: same-sex contact by sexual strictness
(−0.036 vs −0.012 cross-domain), non-marital cohabitation by family strictness (−0.051 vs **+0.002**),
non-marital birth likewise (−0.052 vs **+0.016**). Three of five behaviours show the same-domain count
at least twice the cross-domain one; the other two (early first sex, divorce) do not separate. ⚠ The
two counts correlate at **+0.54**, which widens both standard errors and therefore pushes toward "cannot
tell" rather than toward either answer; and the split into domains is mine. Letting the data carve instead — two principal components over the eight strictness indicators — agrees with my split on only **6 of 8** items, and the two it moves are exactly the two anomalous cells above: it groups *same-sex relations* with the family norms and *divorce* with the sexual ones. Its own second axis looks less like "sex vs family" than like "**when** it is all right" (ages, divorce) versus "**what counts as a family**". Re-running the whole domain test with the data's own clusters instead of mine, however, makes it **worse**, not better: same-domain dominance drops from **3 of 5** behaviours to **1 of 5**. So the axis along which these strictness attitudes correlate in people's heads is **not the way they map onto behaviour**. The domain result stands as I carved it; the reading that the data's second axis is "timing versus family form" is withdrawn.

**Across societies, the same coupling is not one fact but several.** In the SCCS, attitude and
frequency of the *same* practice were coded separately, so condemnation and rarity can be compared
with societies as the unit. For **female premarital sex** they travel together very tightly
(ρ = **+0.797**, n = 97); for **homosexuality** (−0.371, n = 34) and **rape** (−0.519, n = 17) the
sign is the other way, and neither clears the family-wise permutation null. ⚠ The premarital figure
is **not a mechanism claim**: attitude and frequency were coded by the same team from the same
ethnographies, so a single sentence can produce both numbers. That confound is now **bounded rather
than merely named** — inside the same publication, the typical coupling between any two of its
variables is **|ρ| median 0.217**, and the only three pairs tighter than the headline are **the same
question asked of men and of women**. It remains downgraded.

| A conjecture born here cannot be tested here | The six topics split three-down / three-up under a threshold sweep (`#494a`), which suggested that the gap widens where the behaviour leaves a third-party-visible trace. That conjecture was generated from those six slopes, so it cannot be tested on them. Screening every attitude item in both surveys for a usable partner gave **0 of 6** eligible new topics with a matching *own-behaviour* variable — beliefs, other people's rights, and attitudes with no behavioural counterpart. It is frozen, untested, and needs a questionnaire that asks "have you done this", not another pass over these two. |

| "Domain" has no stable referent here | Strictness attitudes predict behaviour better when grouped my way (sexual vs family, 3 of 5 behaviours) than when grouped by how the attitudes correlate (1 of 5). But letting the *behaviour* carve the groups — fitting the carving on one random half and scoring it on the other — gives **three different carvings from three random splits**, agreeing with my split on 6, 4 and 5 of 8 items, and scoring 2 of 5 on held-out data, exactly what my own split scores there. So the grouping that looked like a finding **has no stable referent in this data**: it is a distinction I imposed, it beats a correlational carving, and it cannot be recovered from the behaviour itself. |

| `c3` survives the split-half test that killed a later grouping | Five attempts to name the `c3` direction failed. A later round built a criterion — *do three random half-splits give the same answer* — and used it to retire a grouping elsewhere on this page. Applied back to `c3`: its 32-block sign pattern is **the same answer all three times** (28 of 32 signs agree in every split), against `c1`'s 30 of 32; |cos| between halves is 0.81–0.89. The shuffled floor, **now measured on sixty draws** rather than three, sits at q95 = 0.814 against `c3`'s median of 0.906 over twenty splits — a margin of about three of the statistic's own resolution steps (it can only take values k/32). ⚠ The three-draw estimate had put the floor *higher* (0.838), so the earlier caution erred in the conservative direction. **So the five failed namings are not explained by an unstable direction.** |

| The cross-cultural question cannot be freed from its one coding team | Whether condemnation and rarity travel together *because a society can suppress the behaviour* is the most interesting thing the 186-society file suggested, and it cannot be tested there. Across every source in that file, **only one coding team ever recorded both an attitude and a frequency for the same practice** — the same 1976 project whose shared authorship is exactly the confound at issue. The other sources that code frequencies (four of them) code **warfare and violence**, and their attitude items name no matching practice. So the conjecture is frozen, not refuted: it needs an ethnographic coding built by different hands for the same practices, which does not exist in this release. |

| The headline cannot be replicated anywhere, and the reason is the same everywhere | The first half of this page rests on one pairing: *how uncommon what arouses you is* against *how ashamed you are of it*. Four large public instruments were checked for that pairing. **The GSS, the NSFG and the SCCS carry neither side.** The NSFG's 3,091 variables contain exactly one embarrassment item, and it is about discussing condoms. The Multidimensional Sexual Self-Concept Questionnaire (n = 17,685) carries the *shame* side well — nine of its hundred items are anxiety, depression, blame or fear about one's own sexuality — and **zero of its hundred items ask what the respondent is aroused by**. So of four instruments, one asks about preferences, one asks about shame, and **not one of them asks both**. The claim is not contradicted elsewhere; it is unaskable elsewhere. |

# What the second half was for

The first half of this page is one survey asked once. The second half asked whether any of it survives
an instrument that does not share its method — forty rounds against the GSS (1972–2024), the NSFG
(two ACASI waves), the SCCS (186 ethnographically coded societies) and one psychometric scale
(n = 17,685). What it produced, in one place:

- **A null with content.** In the US, the share saying pornography should be illegal for everyone fell
  0.38 → 0.18 across thirty surveys, and the log-odds gap between condemners and non-condemners in
  reporting having watched it did not move: **Δ = −0.10, CI [−0.29, +0.09], MDE 0.27** `[#486]`.
- **A number that is an artefact of the question.** Randomly split halves of the same sample, asked
  two differently-worded behaviour questions, answer **0.320 vs 0.425** — **Δ = +0.105, CI [+0.079,
  +0.135]** `[#487]`.
- **A gap that survives a different institution and a different mode**, at **−1.22, CI [−1.41,
  −1.05]**, across four admissible cells spanning −1.22 to −1.41 `[#489, #517]`.
- **And a scale that shows the previous line proves less than it looks.** Two questions differing only
  in an age — 18 versus 16 — move the same gap from **−0.52 to −1.07** with disjoint intervals, so a
  cross-instrument agreement of 0.20 is a fifth of what one word does `[#492, #511]`.
- **Strictness is a person-level coordinate, and it is two rulers, not one.** Per additional strict
  position on eight norms, the probability of having done the corresponding thing falls about
  **0.019–0.027**, and the effect is carried by the same domain as the behaviour (three of five) `[#500, #501]`.
- **An eleventh, and it is mostly a warning: men sit close to women, but the same question moved further in six years than it differs between the sexes.** Of the ten items above, the later wave of the same survey kept only two — one sexual, one about family — so the coupling measure cannot be computed across waves at all, and the age-versus-cohort ambiguity in the line above **cannot be resolved with this data** `[#540「波之间做不到」]`. What the two surviving items do allow is a check on the sex of the respondent, which every finding above was silent about: the correlation between them is **0.473** for men and **0.529** for women in the same wave, a gap of 0.056 against a resolution of 0.045 — men slightly lower, but close. ⚠ The same correlation among women was **0.326** six years earlier: a shift of **+0.203**, nearly four times the male–female gap. ⚠ That shift is not safe to read as people changing, because the module those two questions sit in shrank from ten items to two between the waves, and the surrounding items are a known driver of how answers correlate. **The comparison that looked like a bonus is the one carrying the largest uncontrolled effect.**

- **A tenth: sexual morality is already a single block at 15, while family morality is still tightening at 44.** Splitting the same 5,600 women into six age bands, the coupling among the sexual items is flat — 0.445, 0.435, 0.361, 0.394, 0.432, 0.422, a slope of −0.0006 per year against a resolution of 0.0063 — while the coupling among the family items climbs monotonically across all six bands, 0.124 → 0.160, a 29% relative rise `[#539「一个在十五岁就已经成形」]`. The gap between them is itself flat, so the ordering never reverses. ⚠ This is a single survey wave, so age and birth cohort are perfectly collinear: the correct reading is "**with age or with cohort**", and the sentence "people change" is not available from this design at all. ⚠ The band resolution rests on six points, so the family slope's interval is tight only because the six are nearly monotone — the ordering is the finding, not the slope's digits. ⚠ Women only, 15–44, so nothing here speaks about men or about later life.

- **A ninth, in the instrument the previous one lacked: sexual morality is tighter than the family morality asked beside it, in the same format, of the same women.** The line above could not separate *domain* from *format*, because the survey it used has no second four-point non-sexual moral battery. A different survey does. In the NSFG, 5,600 women answered eleven five-point items in one block ⚠ **[CORRECTED: this page previously called that block self-administered; the dictionary places it before the question setting the ACASI language, and the self-interview sections that follow carry `casi`-prefixed variables — so the attitude items were read out by an interviewer, exactly like the GSS ones]** — three about sexual behaviour, seven about divorce, cohabitation and unmarried parenthood. Within the sexual items the median rank correlation is **0.425**; within the family items, **0.140**; across the two, **0.217**. The gap of **+0.285** is about **19×** the bootstrap spread of the two figures, and it survives all three ways of classifying the one ambiguous item (adoption by gay adults) `[#536「性比家庭更紧」]`. ⚠ Because the cross-domain figure sits *between* the two rather than near zero, the two moralities are **not separate blocks** — they are related, and the sexual one is the more internally consistent. ⚠ Three sexual items give only three pairs, so this figure is far less precise than the family one; the comparison is made against a resampling spread for exactly that reason. ⚠ The ceiling here is **0.589**, the correlation between asking about 18-year-olds and 16-year-olds — the same pair of questions whose *difference* was reported further up.

- **An eighth, and it points the other way: there is one ruler at the person level, even though there is none at the level of the society or the era.** Within a single survey year — so no trend can contaminate it — the four sexual-attitude items cohere across individuals at a median rank correlation of **0.375**, against **0.093** for pairs of unrelated attitude items in the same questionnaire ⚠ **and two things about that reference figure were wrong** `[#607「参照那个 0.093,是一堆符号随机的零取了绝对值」]`. First, this page said those items were *answered by the same people*: across the 1,271 available combinations of a sexual pair with a reference pair, **30.7% share not one respondent** — the GSS runs split ballots, and the flagship comparison rests in part on disjoint subsamples. Second, and larger: **70 of 187 reference pairs correlate NEGATIVELY**, while **0 of 113 sexual pairs do**. The reference figure is a median of *absolute* values over correlations whose signs are essentially random — the signed median is **+0.07** — so 0.093 was never a weak coherence. It is the size of nothing, measured at this n. The comparison the claim rests on is therefore **stronger and different from what was written**: the sexual items cohere; the reference items do not cohere at all. ⚠ **And the caveat this claim has carried since it was first made — that GSS has no non-sexual moral battery in the same response format to compare against — has now been settled in a different instrument** `[#608「换一具仪器,那道一直分不开的墙自己让开了」]`. The Moral Foundations Questionnaire asks 30 items across five moral domains, **in one questionnaire, of the same 6,741 people, on one response scale** — none of the split-ballot problem above. Within-domain coherence: **purity 0.499**, authority 0.326, harm 0.316, fairness 0.280, ingroup 0.240, against **0.136** for pairs from different domains and **0.050** for the questionnaire's own nonsense catch item. So the tightness is not an artefact of the sexual items being four-point: **the purity family — the one that contains sexual conduct — is the tightest of the five by 18 times the estimate's own spread**, and it is the **only** one of the five that does not weaken when the response scale changes (0.493 → 0.512 across formats, while harm loses 32% and authority 19%). ⚠ And the rival that could not be tested at all in the GSS — that this is really religion — **is answerable here, because the same people also reported how often they attend religious services.** Removing religious attendance, purity keeps 82% of its coherence while the other four keep 92–103%, and **purity's lead over the next domain falls from +0.171 to +0.087 — about half of it is religion, and about half is not** (+0.087 is 9× its own spread; partialling age instead leaves the lead at 103%, the control that failed outright in the GSS). ⚠ Only **one** of the six purity items names a sexual act, so this is a statement about the purity family, not about sexual morality alone; and these are self-selected internet volunteers, not a probability sample. ⚠ **And the obvious next suspicion — that purity looks tight because one of its six items literally asks about God — is wrong, and wrong in the opposite direction** `[#609「撑住它的不是上帝,也不是贞洁」]`. Removing religious attendance, the God item keeps **78%** of its coherence with the rest of the family while the other five keep **83–93%** and the other four domains keep **97%** — so it does carry more of the religion than its neighbours. But **delete it and purity's lead over the next domain goes UP, not down**: +0.171 → +0.198 raw, and +0.087 → **+0.137** once religion is removed. The same is true of **chastity**, the one item that names a sexual act: delete it and the lead rises to +0.123. The only item whose deletion *lowers* the lead is the plainest one — *whether someone violated standards of purity and decency* — which takes it to +0.068. Across all six single-item deletions purity remains the tightest of the five domains in **7 of 7** variants, before and after religion is removed. **What holds this family together is not God and not chastity; it is the bare sense that some things are indecent, and the two items a critic would reach for first are the two that dilute it.** ⚠ **The obvious objection to that — the decency item is just the vaguest one, and a vague item correlates with everything — was pre-registered and tested, and the pre-registered statistic came back undecided** `[#610「最泛的那道题在别人域里,不在这一族」]`. Its cross-domain correlation ranks **8th of 30**; by median it is level with the top purity item (+0.0001) and by mean it is 0.029 above, and the four specifications split two-two, so on that measure the answer is an interval and not a verdict. But two things settle the direction, and **both are post-hoc rather than pre-registered, which is why they are stated as the next question rather than the answer**: ⛔ **the first of those two was WRONG, and the round that pre-registered it caught it** `[#611「一张按别的列排序的表,读不出这一列的名次」]`. It was read off a table sorted by a *different* column and truncated to twelve rows; ranked properly, decency's within-to-cross ratio of 2.94 is **11th of 30, not 1st**, and the top four are **fairness and harm** items — *treated differently* 4.25, *compassion* 4.12, *justice* 4.08, *acted unfairly* 3.95. The second stands and sharpens: the four **lowest** ratios in the whole questionnaire are all **ingroup** items, ending at *I am proud of my country's history*, **0.96 — it correlates more outside its own domain than inside it.** So the corrected picture is not the one that was written. **Purity is the loudest moral family, not the most separate one**: its items cohere harder than any other domain's (0.499) *and* they spill further into the rest of a person's morality than fairness or harm do. What is most self-contained is **fairness** — a person's view about unfair treatment predicts their other fairness views and almost nothing else. And the attack that motivated all this is dead on its own terms: purity's items are **not** less variable than the rest (SD 1.47 against 1.36), and artificially collapsing every item's top two response categories moves the ratios by **0.95%**, which is what the attenuation algebra predicts, since an item's own reliability cancels out of a ratio of its own correlations. ⚠ **And the new fact that survived all of that — purity coheres hardest and spills furthest — turned out not to mean what it sounded like** `[#612「串起一个人整套道德的,不是恶心,是他把自己放在哪一边」]`. Asking which domain binds the *rest* of morality together — remove one domain's common factor, and how much shared structure is left among the other four — puts purity **second, not first**: authority removes the most (**0.597** of the others' cross-domain correlation survives), then purity 0.765, ingroup 0.790, harm 0.977, and fairness **0.997 — removing what people share about fairness leaves the rest of their morality untouched.** And a single **1-to-7 political self-placement** removes **more** than the whole six-item purity composite does (0.674 vs 0.765, a gap of 0.091 against a resampling spread of 0.037, same sign in all four specifications) — **and almost exactly as much as the first principal component of all thirty moral items (0.674 vs 0.680).** Partialling age leaves 1.001 and pure noise 1.001, so the instrument is not simply deflating everything. **Purity is loud, not central**: what runs through the whole of a person's morality is where they place themselves politically, and among the moral families it is **deference**, not disgust, that holds the others together. ⚠ This is statistical, not causal, and the strongest objection is named rather than answered: a person's political self-placement may be a *summary they compute from* these very moral views, in which case its explanatory power is closer to a tautology than to a cause. ⚠ **That objection was then run as its own round, and it has a sharper answer than either side of it** `[#613「那道政治题不是一个道德维度的简写,是恰好两个的」]`. Removing the first moral factor and *then* politics, politics still takes away **12.4%** of the shared structure on its own — more than a marginal-matched sham (+0.005), more than age (−0.001) or pure noise (+0.000). But removing the first **two** moral factors and then politics, its own contribution falls to **+0.0005 and +0.0012 — indistinguishable from nothing**, while a genuine second factor still contributes +0.035. The split runs cleanly along the principal-component axis of the grid rather than at random, so the reading is not a choice between the two cells: **where a person puts themselves on a single left-right line is not shorthand for one moral dimension; it is shorthand for exactly two, and for nothing outside them.** ⚠ Both orders of removal land on the same residual to the last digit, which is what makes each figure a *unique* contribution rather than an artefact of which was removed first. ⚠ **Trying to name those two dimensions failed, and the shape of the failure is the result** `[#614「两条轴各自不是一个域,是一捆域 —— 而这一句是对仪器的验证,不是发现」]`. The axes themselves are solid: the first two eigenvalues are 6.81 and 4.00, a 41% gap, and each axis reproduces itself across resampling at |cos| 0.999. But **no single moral concern can claim either one.** The first axis correlates 0.896 with authority, 0.885 with purity and 0.808 with loyalty — a spread of 0.010 between the top two — and the second correlates 0.877 with harm and 0.809 with fairness, a spread of 0.068. **A random direction in the same 30-dimensional space produces a top-two spread of 0.093 ± 0.077**, so both observed spreads sit *below* their own null: this design cannot certify a name, and nothing in it supports one. Each axis is a **bundle** — three concerns on the first, two on the second, welded tightly enough that none of them is the axis. ⚠ **And that two-bundle structure is what this questionnaire's own literature reports, so this paragraph is a verification that the instrument behaves as documented, not a discovery.** What is new here is only what it does to the previous claim: a person's political self-placement sits in that plane at **R² = 0.425**, correlating 0.591 with the first bundle and 0.276 with the second, which clears the 0.20 threshold written before the run — so *politics is a summary of exactly two moral dimensions* survives, and now has a picture: **it is mostly where you stand on authority-purity-loyalty, and partly the opposite of where you stand on harm-fairness.** ⚠ **Asked whether those two axes exist outside the questionnaire that produced them, the test came back UNVERIFIED — because the bar I set was higher than the design can reach** `[#615「我把门槛设在了这个设计的天花板之上」]`. The same 8,193 people were also asked, for 26 acts, whether they would do it **for any amount of money**. Sorting those 26 by which axis explains them better gives a picture that needs no statistics to read: **selling your soul (R² 0.149 vs 0.003), burning a flag (0.113 vs 0.000), renouncing your citizenship, cursing the founders** load on the authority-purity-loyalty axis, while **killing an endangered animal (0.099 on the other axis), pledging allegiance to a racist, kicking a dog, mocking the overweight** load on harm-fairness. But the pre-registered test counted how many of the 26 each axis claims, and the count's own noise floor is larger than the counts: two genuinely different moral scores separate 4 and 6 of them, the two axes separate 6 and 4, two random directions separate 0 and 2 — and the threshold, floor plus two standard deviations, came to 4.28, **above what even a real contrast returns.** So the ordering above is a picture and not a verdict, and the defect is in my criterion, not in the instrument. ⚠ Nor would passing have settled the deeper circle: **these 26 scenarios were written by the same authors from the same theory as the 30 items**, so this could only ever have tested whether the axes are an artefact of thirty particular sentences — never whether they are an artefact of the theory. ⚠ **Taking that question back to the society level then overturned something I had written by hand an hour earlier** `[#616「我手读判了零,机器扫描找回了五个 —— 而其中一个是问卷给不了的设计」]`. Reading the four SCCS variables whose titles say *punishment* or *sanctions*, every one turned out to measure something else — **who** punishes, **which sex** is punished more, how coercive the state is, or how often the act happens — and I was about to write that the society level has no harm-side severity scale at all. A mechanical sweep of all 1,781 variables, validated by requiring it to recover three severity scales I had confirmed by hand and to return nothing from the economy category, found **five**: how a society judges **rape**, and — on one four-point scale, for the *same* act — **the acceptability of physical violence toward one's own local community, toward one's society beyond it, and toward other peoples.** ⚠ The sweep is not clean either: of its fifteen hits I hand-checked every one and **three are false positives**, all on the other side (two code *who* is punished, one codes whether a name taboo exists), so the honest counts are **seven and five**, not ten and five. **The wall I nearly wrote onto this page did not exist** — and what is behind it is a shape no person-level questionnaire in this project has offered: one act, one scale, three widening circles of who it is done to. ⚠ Two corrections to that sentence before the result: the local-community item runs on **three** levels, not four — it has no *Valued* option where the other two do — and the coverage is **87 societies, not 186**, because the figure I quoted was a row count and not a count of societies actually coded. Running the separator anyway `[#617「一个社会不决定暴力对不对,它决定对谁做才不对」]`: **acceptability climbs monotonically with distance — 1.37 inside your own community, 2.33 for your society beyond it, 3.38 for other peoples** (ρ = +0.654, block-bootstrapped over 10°×10° grid cells, CI [+0.601, +0.711]) against a same-coding-team background coupling of 0.187 ± 0.152 and a placebo of 0.043. Collapsing all three items to the same three levels — the control for the scale asymmetry — costs **0.041 of the 0.654**, about 6%. And **a second ethnographic team, coding fifteen years later, on their own three-level scale running in the opposite direction, finds the same climb on 75 societies**: 1.31 → 1.66 → 2.33, ρ = +0.470 [+0.376, +0.564]. Two of the three specifications clear the bar written before the run; the independent replication sits just under it with an interval that contains it, so this is reported as an interval and not a verdict. **A society does not decide whether violence is wrong. It decides whom it is wrong to do it to.** ⚠ **The obvious next question — does sexual condemnation have the same shape, graded by who the partner is — is still unanswered, and the reason is worth more than the answer would have been** `[#618「三个搜索版本,没有一个能找回它自己已知的正例」]`. Three searches were built to census it. The first read only the response codes and missed the violence triple entirely, because **its 'to whom' lives in the variable's title, not in its codes.** The second added titles and still failed, because **each of those variables carries only one target** — the dimension exists across three variables, not inside one, so a rule demanding two targets per variable is blind to exactly the design it is hunting. The third searched by family — variables sharing a stem and differing only in the target phrase — and recovered four of six, defeated by trailing qualifiers after a comma. **Every version failed its own positive control, so every zero it produced is inadmissible**, and the honest state of the sexual side is **unmeasured, not absent**. What the round did establish is a correction: the seven sexual-severity variables I reported at n = 186 are actually coded for **130, 109, 40, 29, 61, 51 and 54 societies** — that figure was a row count, and rows include the ones whose code is blank.

The abortion battery reaches **0.437**, which is as close to a repeated question as this instrument offers. So a person's severity about one sexual practice does predict their severity about the others, at roughly **86%** of the coherence of a near-duplicate battery, and that figure does not move across 51 years (slope −0.001/year against a resolution of 0.002) `[#534「三个单位,三个答案」]`. ⚠ The reference items are mostly binary while the sexual items are four-point, and a binary pair is mechanically less correlated. Collapsing the sexual items to binary to match, the excess falls to **+0.238** at the strict cutpoint and **+0.151** at the broad one, against a reference spread of 0.036 — so scale length accounts for **13% to 45%** of it and the honest figure is an interval, **between +0.15 and +0.27** `[#535「格式解释掉一部分,不是全部」]`. The cheapest check settles the direction: the abortion battery *is* binary and still reaches **0.439**, so being binary does not cap a correlation at 0.09. ⚠ What remains unseparated is content: the reference items — capital punishment, marijuana, euthanasia — do not belong to one moral domain the way the four sexual items do, and this instrument has no second four-point non-sexual moral battery to fix that with. ⚠ Taken with the two findings above, the pattern is that **an individual has a single sexual morality; a society and an era do not** — which is what one would expect if a society is an aggregate of institutions with separate histories rather than a large person.

- **A seventh, in a different country and a different unit: what looks like a shared climate is mostly a shared trend.** American attitudes to homosexuality, premarital sex, extramarital sex and teen sex all softened across 51 years, and *as levels* they correlate at a median of **+0.82** — the textbook spurious correlation between two series that both drift. Asking instead whether they move together *in the same interval*, and matching the noise structure so the comparison is fair, gives **+0.24**, against **+0.585** for two halves of the population answering the *same* question, and against a placebo band reaching **0.436** when the years are shuffled `[#532「一件一件松开」]`. So cross-topic movement is about **41%** of within-topic movement and is **not separable from noise at this resolution** — the honest statement is a ratio and a limit, not a zero. ⚠ That 41% is for movement between *adjacent* surveys, and the regime matters: widening the window to 6, 12 and 18 years raises the ratio to 0.44, 0.52, 0.76. That looks like signal emerging from noise, and it was run for exactly that reason — but it **stops being a separator** as the window grows, because a difference taken across 18 years of a 51-year one-way trend is nearly the trend itself, and the trend is the thing already known to correlate at +0.82. The rise is therefore consistent with both explanations and settles neither `[#533「长滞后不是分离器」]`. ⚠ The two series were built from **disjoint respondents** (men's answers to one item against women's to the other), which removes the shared-questionnaire artefact but cannot remove a shared cause in the world; that is the thing being measured.

- **A sixth: there is no such thing as a "strict society" in this data.** If harshness were a property of a society rather than of a practice, societies severe about one sexual practice would be severe about the others. On the best-powered pair available — how harshly a society judges premarital sex against how harshly it judges extramarital sex, **n = 90** — the rank correlation is **+0.086**, against a resolution of 0.31. Across the whole eight-cell grid the median is **+0.125** — and the right thing to compare that against is not zero but how much any two variables from this same coding team correlate anyway. Built properly, from ordinal variables of the *same kind* spanning *different* practices, that baseline is **+0.187 with a resolution of 0.152**, so **+0.125 is indistinguishable from the coupling this instrument produces on its own** `[#529「最有力的那一对,几乎是零」]` `[#530「不可分辨,不是低于」]`. ⚠ An earlier wording here said +0.125 was *below* a baseline of 0.224; that baseline was borrowed from a different set of variable pairs and has been replaced. The split is real — two measures of the *same* practice correlate at **0.859**, four and a half times the cross-practice figure. Assigning variables to practices is a judgement, so it was varied: the baseline moves by **0.004** across schemes and the verdict does not change `[#531「指派不是承重墙」]`. ⚠ Of three schemes written down, only **two distinct schemes** actually ran — one merge changed nothing because the pair it would have merged never had enough overlapping societies to enter. ⚠ Two specifications do clear that baseline (+0.359, +0.361) — but each does so by **dropping the one well-powered cell** and collapsing the grid onto a single variable's row, so the whole curve is published here rather than the cell that flatters it. ⚠ And one variable had to be discarded before any of this: `SCCS172` "Wifesharing" reads like a permissiveness scale from its title, but its codes are *circumstances* — for the husband's economic benefit, toward a specific group — a nominal typology. It was carrying two of the three best-powered pairs.

- **And a fifth: across 186 societies, "rarer is more condemned" holds in exactly one of five cells.** The Standard Cross-Cultural Sample codes both the attitude to a practice and its frequency, for premarital sex, extramarital sex and homosexuality. Rank-correlating condemnation against rarity gives **+0.80 (n=97)** for premarital sex among women and **+0.63 (n=91)** among men — but **+0.21 and +0.10** for extramarital sex and **−0.37** for homosexuality, all three of them inside their own resolution and therefore invisible. So the relation is not a law about practices; it is a fact about one practice `[#528「不是关于做法的定律」]`. ⚠ And the instrument bites: pairs of variables coded by the **same team** are **2.14×** more correlated than pairs spanning two teams (median |ρ| 0.224 vs 0.105, 29 and 319 pairs), so part of any such number is the ethnographer's handwriting. The premarital figure survives that comparison — it exceeds all 15 same-source pairs and their median of 0.31 — but with 15 pairs only the median is resolvable, never the tail.

- **A twelfth, and it is mostly a correction of scope: "sex is one block" is scale-dependent, and the check that shows it is a verification, not a discovery.** In a self-selected online battery of 17,685 people, the 100 sexual self-concept items split cleanly: two items about the *same* aspect of a person's sexuality correlate at a median of **0.579**, two items about *different* aspects at **0.173**, and randomly regrouping the same 100 items gives a gap whose 95th percentile is **0.019** — the real gap is 0.406 `[#542「面内高于面间,而那是设计」]`. ⚠ But those groups are the questionnaire author's own design, and items inside one are near-paraphrases: "I am very aware of my sexual feelings and needs" against "I am very aware of my sexual motivations and desires" correlate at 0.666. **A result that restates how an instrument was built is a verification that it works, and calling it a finding about people would be the same error this page has caught elsewhere.** What it legitimately establishes is the scope of the earlier bullets: the block-ness measured in the national surveys is a property of **moral judgements about practices**, and says nothing about whether a person's sexual *self-concept* is unified — at that scale it plainly is not.

- **Two more instruments were opened to test the above against a different source, and both are structurally unable to answer it.** Everything in the three findings above comes from surveys run by one government statistical system. A self-selected online battery of 100 sexual self-concept items has **no non-sexual items at all**, so the sexual-versus-other comparison cannot exist there; and a right-wing authoritarianism dataset of 9,881 respondents ships **no item texts at all** — the codebook names its twenty-two questions only by number, pointing at a book. Deciding which of them are sexual-morality items would mean classifying from memory, and an estimand built on a remembered classification is not evidence about anything. A third dataset — the moral-foundations study of 8,193 people that was sitting unopened in this project's own download folder — ships five scale scores and no item texts either. Written a round earlier, that read **"three datasets, three times the same gap"** — and a tool built the next round to check all twenty sources at once **withdrew the sentence**: the sexual self-concept battery ships its hundred item texts in full, so its gap is a *missing comparison*, not a missing meaning, and the two were being counted as one. The tool was then pointed at all twenty sources, and it revised its own verdict four times: each time it reported a source as having no item texts, looking at that source showed the texts were there in an encoding it had not anticipated — embedded in a Stata file, in a CSV column, behind alphanumeric item codes, inside a JSON block, inside a SAS label statement. Seven such reversals in, the conclusion stopped being another pattern and became a rule: **no finite set of parsers licenses the word "absent"**, so the word was removed from the tool. It now reports item semantics **present in nine sources and undecidable in eleven, and absent in none — because it can no longer say absent** `[#545「工具当场撤回了我上一轮的句子」]` `[#546「没有任何有限的解析器集合能许可」]`. A second tool then asked the question the first one cannot — whether a label actually describes its column — and found that within a single questionnaire, **"yes/no" is encoded three different ways**: `1/2`, `0/1`, and `1/5`. Two variables whose labels begin "Ever had…" take the values 1, 5 and 7, and nothing in the label says so; reading them as zero-and-one, which is what the wording invites, silently inverts the answer. That is not a hypothetical — it cost this project a retracted result before the check existed. Swept across every labelled variable in both national surveys, the pattern turns out to be the rule rather than the exception in one of them: **83% of the NSFG variables whose label begins "Whether" or "Ever" are coded 1-and-5, not 0-and-1** (54 such variables in the other survey, 11% of them). A pre-registered rule said that any source above 5% forces a recheck of every variable this project has used from it; both tripped it, the recheck ran, and it found **no further misreadings** — the two affected variables had already been mapped explicitly and guarded by a base-rate assertion `[#548「触发了,而重查是干净的」]`. ⚠ The first version of the check reported 73%, and inspection showed most of those were five-point items whose *question wording* happened to contain the word "has". A trigger fired by an instrument whose specificity has never been measured is not a trigger. The guard that caught all of this — print a column's base rate and require it to fall inside a range written down beforehand — was being hand-written once per round, which is how a guard eventually gets skipped; it is now a library function that refuses to run at all unless the caller states how the column's codes were mapped `[#549「「我知道它的码」正是每一次读错时我以为的事」]`. ⚠ It cannot certify a mapping is right: a column read backwards whose base rate happens to sit near one half would pass, and that sentence is printed in its own output rather than kept in a footnote. The four checks were then chained into one report and pointed at a health survey this project has never used — 1.2 GB, 445,000 respondents — and the report's first two sections came back **empty**: the format carries no variable labels, so not one of the four levels can say anything about it, and it says so instead of producing a verdict `[#550「一件在陌生对象面前会沉默的工具」]`. ⚠ Getting that far required noticing that the file inside the archive is named with a **trailing space**, which is why every earlier attempt to find it by extension had silently found nothing. What *can* be said without a codebook is the shape, and one pass over the file gives it: **433,323 respondents, 350 columns, and a median column that is 49% empty**. A hundred and twelve columns are more than 90% empty, and eight of them are **entirely** empty — present in the file, carrying nothing `[#551「列在文件里,一个值也没有」]`. Most of that is almost certainly the survey's state-optional modules rather than damage, **but without the codebook those two cannot be told apart** — so the file was asked directly: if those columns were state-optional, their emptiness should cluster by state. Across 52 states and one pass of the file, **none of the 112 does** (92 are state-independent, 20 undecided), against controls that behave — 62 fully-complete columns show zero between-state variance, and shuffling the state labels collapses everything `[#552「我的世界分解里少了一个」]`. ⚠ The verdict is clean and the **question was malformed**: this survey also splits questionnaire versions *within* a state, and a module given to half the sample in every state produces exactly this pattern. A third possibility predicts the same observation as the two being compared, which means the test never separated them. The guess that they were "almost certainly state modules" was mine, and it was written a round before anything checked it.

- **And one sentence carried over from a result that was not published: five "different" sexual behaviours in a 1.5-million-row adolescent survey are not five behaviours.** Ever had sex, sex before 13, number of partners, currently active, condom use — across seventeen survey years they move together at **+0.885** against an instrument ceiling of 0.882, which is to say perfectly. A pre-registered check then asked whether that synchrony extends to sexual violence (sexual, but not downstream of being sexually active) or to injecting drug use (risky, but not sexual): **it extends to neither** (−0.039 and +0.264, both inside the noise band of 0.510). So the near-perfect figure is one quantity measured five ways, and the rule written before the run said that such a result does not go on this page as a finding. ⚠ A later round found that the +0.885 was a **median over a sign-inconsistent set**: one of the five items codes 1 as *yes, ever had sex* while the other four code 1 as the low-risk answer, so it correlates at **−0.86** with each of them while they correlate at **+0.89** with each other. All ten magnitudes sit between 0.85 and 0.89 — the underlying claim survives and is if anything stronger — but **the median hid a flipped sign, and a median of signed correlations across items whose coding direction was never checked should not have been reported at all** `[#556「中位把一个符号翻转藏起来了」]`. Recomputed with every item pointed the same way — the direction read out of the survey's own format library rather than guessed — the ten values run from +0.851 to +0.894 and the median is unchanged at **+0.885**.

- **A thirteenth, and the answerable version of the question the sexual items could not carry: adolescent risk behaviours move one domain at a time.** Sorting thirteen items into five domains that are not downstream of one another — sexual activity, violence, substances, traffic safety, mental health — and asking whether they move together across seventeen survey years: **within a domain the median is +0.555, across domains it is −0.117**, against an instrument ceiling of 0.843 and a noise band reaching 0.491. Between-domain movement is **inside the noise**; within-domain movement is well above it `[#557「一个领域一个领域地动」]`. ⚠⚠ **[DOWNGRADED — the judgement this rests on turns out to carry it entirely.]** Which domain an item belongs to was read off its question text by one person, and re-sorting the same thirteen items three defensible ways moves the within-minus-between gap from **+0.672** to **+0.760** to **−0.092** — the sign flips when mental-health items are grouped with substance use, which is a grouping the comorbidity literature would not call strange. The spread across sortings is **0.85**, bootstrap interval [0.34, 1.36], against a pre-registered tolerance of 0.05 `[#575「换一种分法,符号就翻了」]`. **So the sentence to keep is not "behaviours move one domain at a time" but "whether they do depends on where one draws the domains, and this data cannot draw them for you."** ⚠ **[The cross-unit summary that stood here is withdrawn.]** It read *"one person carries a single ruler, while history moves one domain at a time"* — and the second half no longer holds, because the domains were mine. What survives is the first half, and it survives a weaker test than the sentence implied: re-sorting the person-level items three defensible ways keeps the sign in every sorting (+0.198, +0.263, +0.285) but moves the size by **0.087**, about a third of the effect. So sexual morality is tighter than family morality **in every way of drawing the line that was tried**, and the number attached to it is a range, not a point `[#576「方向活下来,数字没有」]`. ⚠ That question — **does the answer depend on which items I called the same kind of thing?** — was asked four times on this page's grouped findings, and it changed the answer three times: the spread across defensible re-sortings was 0.004, then 0.038 on a smaller pool, then **0.85 with a sign flip**, then 0.087. **None of it ever appeared in an interval, a minimum detectable effect, or a control.** It is now a library function with its own two controls — an identity re-sorting must return "not load-bearing", a constructed flip must return "sign reversal" — and **every grouped claim on this page is expected to report it** `[#577「分类是判断最多的一步」]`. ⚠ Applied to the one cross-unit sentence still standing — *a person carries a single sexual morality* — on **a second survey**, it holds: sorting the General Social Survey's items three defensible ways gives **+0.238, +0.218, +0.201**, positive every time, spread **0.037** `[#579「两份仪器,两次都问过这一刀」]`. **That makes it the only claim here that has been asked "does this depend on how you grouped things?" on two different instruments and answered no on both.** ⚠ The spread's own interval, [0.008, 0.086], straddles the tolerance, so what survives cleanly is the **direction**, not the verdict that grouping is irrelevant. ⚠ And the objection that had been open since the format problem was first named — that the sexual items are four- or five-point while the comparison items are yes/no, so the two groups carry different amounts of information — is now closed on the one survey where it can be: in the NSFG both sets are five-point, so both can be reduced to yes/no together. Done that way the gap is **+0.264** at the strict cut and **+0.271** at the broad one, against **+0.285** with everything left at five points — **93% and 95% of it survives making the formats identical** `[#580「格式不是解释」]`. Building the asymmetry back in deliberately, in the opposite direction, moves it to **+0.201**, which is what the format is worth: about 0.07, and it does not account for the finding. ⚠ That episode prompted a sweep of every "this cannot be done" clause this project has written: **51 of them**, sorted by whether they are things the local data could actually settle. **Seven need something that is not here** — a second coding team, an intervention, a second site. **Eight are things that could have been measured and were not**, and the one of those eight that has now been measured turned out to be a **1.4× coefficient rather than a wall** `[#582「五十一条不可能,八条本可以量」]`. The remaining thirty-six the classifier cannot decide, and it says so rather than guessing. **A limitation one writes about one's own work is the kind least likely to be audited, because it feels like honesty rather than like a claim.** ⚠ One of the eight was then measured — chosen by a rule fixed before looking, the one later entries cite most. It said the fertility survey stops at 44 and therefore says nothing about older people. The other survey covers every adult age, so the question has an answer: the coupling among sexual attitudes runs **0.349 · 0.382 · 0.353 · 0.396 · 0.407** across five age bands from eighteen to eighty-nine, and the over-45 half sits **0.031** above the under-45 half against a resolution of 0.021 `[#583「墙后面有东西,而它有大小」]`. **So the wall was real and it is small**: older people's sexual attitudes do hang together more tightly, by about eight percent, and the profile is not a clean gradient — the 45–59 band dips below the 30–44 one before the oldest two rise. ⚠ Four of the eight have now been measured, and **only one turned out to be what it said it was**: one collapsed (a "resolution far below" that is a 1.4× coefficient), one shrank (this one), one had its **direction reversed** — a warning that overlapping windows inflate a noise band, where they in fact shrink it, 0.46 against 0.76 — and one held: reducing five-point items to yes/no costs about **14% of their internal reliability**, so the earlier "93% survives" needs no discount `[#585「四堵墙,只有一堵是它自己说的那样」]`. ⚠ Getting that number required noticing that three of the family items are worded backwards — *people cannot be happy without children*, *a couple should not live together unless married* — and that adding them up unreversed had been halving the scale's reliability. Reversing them by the data and reversing them by reading the question texts pick out **the same two items, eleven for eleven** `[#586「两种方法挑出同样两道反向题」]`. **Summing questions that point opposite ways does not produce a blunter measurement; it produces a quieter one**, which reads as insensitivity rather than as error. ⚠ A sixth wall was opened and turned out to have two halves that deserve different verdicts. "A self-selected online sample cannot be extrapolated to a population" is genuinely structural — there is no probability sample of the same construct anywhere in this collection to anchor it. But the clause continued "…and selection inflates all correlations", and **that direction was asserted and never established**: self-selection can just as easily depress a correlation, by piling respondents at both extremes `[#587「我断言了一个方向,而没有证据」]`. It only ever appeared as a caution, which made every conclusion using it more conservative, so nothing downstream changes — but the sentence has been corrected to say the direction is unknown.

- **A fourteenth, and it is the last question this claim had not been asked: her sexual morality is not a sealed compartment — it reaches further into her other judgements than those judgements reach each other.** Within a single survey year, the four sexual attitude items cohere at **0.359**; five unrelated moral items — the death penalty, marijuana, euthanasia, suicide, women in politics — cohere among themselves at **0.122**; and **across the two groups the figure is 0.192**, higher than the non-sexual items manage with each other `[#588「跨两者比非性内还高」]`. ⚠ Read carefully: those five are a convenience set, not a domain, so their low internal figure is expected and the comparison says as much about them as about any bridge. What the ordering does support is that **the sexual items are the coherent core here**, and that a person's position on them is not stored away from the rest of what she thinks is wrong. ⚠ Repeated on the other survey, where the comparison group **is** a real domain — divorce, cohabitation, unmarried parenthood — the same ordering holds: sexual items cohere most, the cross-group figure comes next, the second domain's internal figure last. But the margin is **1.13 to 1.66** depending on whether the items are read at five points or reduced to yes/no, and the yes/no reading — the one that makes the two surveys comparable at all — gives the smaller number `[#589「次序成立,而余量随口径变」]`. ⚠⚠ **[DOWNGRADED one round later — the reading above was an artifact of the comparison set.]** Swapping in three different second domains, chosen to span loose-to-tight, shows what actually drives that ratio: the **cross-group figure barely moves — 0.194, 0.251, 0.261** — while the second domain's internal coherence moves four times as far — **0.125, 0.435, 0.545**. Against a loose comparison set the cross figure looks larger than it; against a real tight one (the abortion battery, or the euthanasia–suicide pair) it is clearly smaller `[#590「跨几乎不动,动的是分母」]`. **So there is no bridge — what there is is a constant.** A person's position on sexual morality predicts her position on any other moral question at roughly **0.19 to 0.26**, and that number is strikingly indifferent to which other question is asked. Sexual judgements are the tightest cluster these surveys contain; they are **not** unusually well connected to the rest. ⚠ That constant was then attacked with the two explanations that would make it uninteresting. Removing how liberal or conservative she says she is accounts for **12%** of it; removing her habit of choosing the end-points of scales accounts for **28%**; after both, the link still stands at **0.15 to 0.21** against a placebo of 0.008 `[#591「两个解释都不够」]`. **So after subtracting her politics and her answering style, a woman's position on sexual morality still predicts her position on the death penalty, marijuana, abortion and euthanasia by about the same modest amount — and nothing in this data says what does the connecting.** ⚠ The political self-rating is itself an opinion these very attitudes may produce, so removing it may have removed part of what needed explaining; that cannot be ruled out here. ⚠ Two more questions were then put to it, and the second changes what kind of thing it is. **It is not carried by one or two questions**: dropping any single item moves it by at most **15.7%**, and dropping any of the comparison items by at most 6.1%. **And it is not constant** — across fifty-two years the link rises from **0.227 in 1972 to 0.301 in 2024**, a slope that clears its own resolution `[#592「它不是常数,它在长大」]`. **So over half a century, how a person judges sex has become a steadily better predictor of how she judges the death penalty, marijuana and abortion.** The moral space did not stay the same shape while opinions moved inside it; **it tightened around this one point.** ⚠ The slope clears its resolution by only 15%, and the number of usable years differs between items, so the direction is the finding and the rate is not. ⚠ Asked whether that tightening is generations replacing one another or people changing as they age, the answer is **both margins move and this design cannot choose between them**: within a birth cohort the link rises **+0.0019 per year of age**, and within an age band it rises **+0.0016 per year of birth**, each clearing its own resolution, and shuffling birth years collapses the second to 1% of itself `[#593「两个方向都动,而它们不可分」]`. ⚠ This is not a gap that more data would close: age plus birth year **is** survey year, so the three can never be told apart in a single cross-section, and no one in this survey was ever asked twice. **What can be said is that the tightening shows up at both the older end and the later-born end, and that whichever way it works, it has been working in one direction for fifty years.** ⚠ Three of those four cells now rest on more than one instrument. The fourth — attitudes over decades — rests on exactly one, and a check of every other source in this collection found the reason: **none of the fifteen non-government datasets here carries a year dimension at all** `[#558「十五个来源,零个有年份」]`. Self-selected online batteries are collected once; replication archives ship a single study. A survey that asks the same attitude question of a fresh sample every two years for fifty-one years is a rare and expensive object, and where the finding is weakest — the one cell that came back "not separable from noise" — is exactly where no second one exists to check it against. ⚠ The near-miss is worth stating: the step written down next was a feasibility study for collecting new data, and the inventory that preceded it found a directly relevant dataset already on disk. **Counting what you already have is cheaper than every alternative, and it is the step most often skipped.**

The five things this work established it cannot do are not sample-size problems; each is a property of how
the instrument was built. Written as specifications rather than complaints, they are short:

Those are precisely the two that the structural check had already singled out as **not optional**. ⚠ A third component, a probability sampling frame, also shows zero — but that zero means *never verified inside this project*, not *absent*: the sampling design of these surveys was never checked here against the object, and the table refuses to record an impression as a fact. ⚠ One process rule on this project had been written down twice and broken twice — *edit the page and write the ledger in separate steps, and if the page edit fails, do not write the ledger* — so it is now a check rather than a resolution: **from entry 600 onward, a ledger entry must either appear as an anchor on this page or say in so many words that it was not meant to** `[#600「一条只靠记性执行的规矩,它的失效是默认状态」]`. ⚠ The first version of that check could not catch the failure that prompted it, and was thrown away: it looked for entries *claiming* a page edit, and the entry in question never claimed one — it simply assumed it. The version that runs inverts the default instead, and was kept only after it was shown to catch that entry. ⚠ That check, and the nine others this project has built, all had the same defect: **nothing ever ran them except me remembering to** `[#601「十件工具六条规则,而没有一件东西会自己跑」]`. They are now attached to the point where a commit is made, and what that attachment can see was measured rather than assumed: it catches an uncited number added to the English page, an anchor deleted from it, and an anchor pointing at an entry that does not exist — and **it is blind to an uncited number added to the Chinese page, and to an anchor deleted from it.** This page is published in two languages and the guard covers one and a half of them. That has been fixed, and fixing it found something: two of the four rules had the English page's name written into them as a default, so the Chinese page had never been scanned at all — and when it finally was, **eight numbers on it turned out to carry no source** `[#602「一把只量了半个交付物的尺子,它报的合格是一句关于它自己的话」]`. They had been there the whole time, on a published page, in front of anyone who reads Chinese. Nothing had gone wrong; nothing had ever looked. The count this page's guard reports therefore rose from nine to seventeen the moment the instrument stopped being half-blind, and **that is not the page getting worse — it is the number finally being about the page rather than about the ruler.** ⚠ **And that sentence was itself wrong within the hour** `[#603「同一批债被数了两遍,而我把它写成了另有八笔债」]`: every one of those eight paragraphs turned out to be the *same* claim that was already uncited in English. The debt did not double when the instrument stopped being half-blind — **the counting did.** There are **nine** distinct claims on this page carrying numbers with no source, eight of which are simply counted once in each language. The guard still counts instances, because a claim has to be repaired in both languages and repairing one is not repairing it; but the number quoted about the page is now the number of claims. The first of the nine has been paid, and paying it produced the sharper result `[#604「一个覆盖不是一个出处,而账本里早就写着正确答案」]`: the numbers in these paragraphs do exist in the ledger — **fifty-five of fifty-six of them** — but scattered across different entries, so no single entry is the source and a search that picks the entry covering the most of them picks confidently and wrongly. For the one repaired here it proposed entry 526, which is the entry recording that this page's anchors had *already* been pointed at the wrong entries once; the correct pair was written down in entry 524 the day the question was first asked. **The provenance was not missing. It was recorded, and never read.** ⚠ One round later the guard refused a commit by the person who wrote it `[#605「我给棘轮定基线的那一刻,页面正处在它最差的状态」]`, and it was right to: the baseline it measures itself against had been frozen in the seconds between this page citing an entry and that entry being written, so the worst moment the page ever passes through had been recorded as its normal condition. **A standard is a photograph, and it inherits whatever the shutter happened to catch.** ⚠ The same question asked of this project's own filing produced the sharpest version of it `[#606「我把每一次信念更新都叫成了一个决定,于是我有 263 个决定和 0 个弧」]`: rounds here are grouped into arcs, an arc is supposed to close when a decision becomes safe, and **123 of the 263 arcs contained exactly one round** — which by the rule that defines them means they were never arcs at all. Measured against a sibling project built to the same standard, its arcs hold about thirty rounds each and this one held two. The 635 rounds are unchanged and renumbered nothing; they now sit in **sixteen** arcs, and what changed is not the work but **what this project was willing to call a decision.** ⚠ The first attempt to prove the guard worked proved nothing twice over: the commit it blocked was blocked by an unrelated rule about commit messages, and the violation it was given to detect was one the guard cannot see, so its silence and its absence looked identical.**

**Nothing in that table is expensive to describe and every row is expensive to build**, which is the actual
finding of the second half: the limit on this question is not what has been analysed, it is what was ever
collected, and by whom, and whether anyone kept asking.


### How one sentence got smaller six times

The claim below is the only one in this project that survived every attack made on it, and it is worth
reading as a sequence rather than a result, because **each version was killed by a test, not replaced by a
better phrase**. What is named at each step is the test, not the conclusion.

| # `[#594「六步,每一步都被一个检验杀掉」]` | The sentence at that moment | What killed it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sexual attitudes are a bridge: they reach further into other moral judgements than those judgements reach each other (0.192 vs 0.122) | — |
| 2 | **Swap the comparison group** for three domains spanning loose-to-tight | The cross figure barely moved (0.194 · 0.251 · 0.261) while the comparison group's internal coherence moved four times as far (0.125 · 0.435 · 0.545). **No bridge — the ratio was the denominator.** |
| 3 | Then it is a constant: sexual position predicts any other moral position at ~0.19–0.26, indifferent to which | — |
| 4 | **Partial out the two explanations that would make it dull** — political self-rating, and the habit of picking scale end-points | They account for **12%** and **28%**; the link survives at 0.15–0.21 against a placebo of 0.008. **Not explained.** |
| 5 | **Drop each item in turn; then plot it by year** | No item carries it (max 15.7%), and it is **not a constant**: 0.227 in 1972 → 0.301 in 2024. **It is a process.** |
| 6 | **Split by age band × birth cohort; then re-band three ways** | Both margins rise (+0.0019 per year of age, +0.0016 per year of birth) and **cannot be told apart** — age plus birth year *is* survey year. Re-banding leaves both signs intact; the cohort margin is the steadier of the two (spread 0.0004 against the age margin's 0.0005, and a placebo that collapses to 1% rather than 26%). |

**Where it ends:** how a person judges sex has become a steadily better predictor of how she judges the
death penalty, marijuana and abortion, across fifty-two years; the tightening appears at both the older
and the later-born end; nothing here says which, and nothing here can, because no one in this survey was
ever asked twice `[#594「六步,每一步都被一个检验杀掉」]`.

## Layout

**E · A · R** — an *epoch* closes when the ontology shifts (the object turned out to be a different
object), an *arc* closes when a decision becomes safe, a *round* closes on one belief update.
**One epoch, 25 arcs, 213 rounds.** `R` is numbered consecutively across the whole project, not
restarted per arc. Every count is discovered, never chosen; see `~/.claude/CLAUDE.md` §P16.

Each round is a directory with `run.py`, `README.md` and `results/`.
`lib/rounds.py` maps a round name to its path, because several rounds reuse an earlier round's
loaders and that dependency is made explicit rather than hidden. Environment is a self-contained
`.venv` (system python 3.14 has no `ensurepip`; pip was bootstrapped).

## Scope, stated once

Cross-sectional · one instrument · one population (18–32, US/Canada/Europe) · aggressively
anonymised, with correlations attenuated roughly 25% by design · every measure self-report.
No causal claim in this repository is identified, and none is made.
